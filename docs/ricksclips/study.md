# Study: @RicksClips3000 — how the last 20 Shorts are written

Source: the 20 most recent explainer Shorts (Oct 17 – Nov 15, 2026). Full cleaned transcripts with
timestamps are in `transcripts.md` next to this file. Older uploads on the channel are comic dubs
("I only do the voice"), so they were skipped.

> **What this covers and what it doesn't.** Sections 1–6 cover the **writing**, from all 20 transcripts.
> Section 7 covers the **look**, from the still frames YouTube publishes for the top 5 videos
> (Jar, Railroads, Japan Job, Storm Oil, Grave Bell): about 3 different frames per video, 15 in all.
> Stills show style, lighting, color, composition and captions. They **can't** show motion, camera
> moves, cut rhythm, transitions or sound. Those still need the actual mp4s (see section 8).

## 2. The skeleton (nearly every video follows it)

Timing is shown as a share of the runtime, so it scales.

| Beat | When | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Absurd act** | 0–5% (first sentence, under 3 s) | A faceless "He / They" does something that looks **irrational, backwards or self-destructive**, stated as plain fact with a concrete detail | "He loaded the shotgun with gold and fired it into the walls of the mine." |
| **2. "You see," context** | 3–15% | Quick world-building: who, where, what's at stake. Usually opens with "You see," | "You see, two companies are racing to build a railroad along a canyon so deep you can't see the bottom." |
| **3. The mockery** | 10–40% | Someone voices the viewer's confusion **as a joke at the hero's expense**. The hero is laughed at, doubted or ignored | "Are we fighting them or grating cheese?" / "Barbie's going to love it in here." |
| **4. Escalation** | 30–60% | Stakes rise in steps tied to **time markers** (next morning, that night, in May / by July, at 15 min / at 20). Usually there's a rival doing it the "normal" way | "In May, you can fit a coin in them. By July, barely a sheet of paper." |
| **5. False "you were wrong" beat** | ~55–65% | The doubter gloats once more, right before the turn | "See? Even your gaps know you were wrong." |
| **6. The mechanism** | **60–85%** | The real reason, in 2–4 sentences, often through an **everyday analogy**. Often opens with a second "You see," | "Ever blown on a bowl of soup? The broth ripples, but the oily spots stay perfectly smooth…" |
| **7. Payoff image** | last 5–10% | One last visual consequence that proves it, usually **ironic**: the doubter or rival pays. Then it just ends | "…the perfect railroad kicks sideways like a snake right as a train rolls onto it." |

**The core move: the "why" is withheld for about 40 seconds.** The viewer sees the strange act at
second 0 and gets the explanation around second 40–50. Everything in between is story that keeps
raising the question.

## 3. Line-level techniques (with counts)

1. **Open on an action, not a question.** 14 of 20 open with "He/They/A man/A knight + past-tense
   action." The rest open on a **counter-intuitive claim** ("If you plant a tree beside an empty
   road, cars will steer right into it"), a **forbidden object** ("a button… nobody's allowed to press it"), a
   **paradox rule** ("never draw first") or a **direct challenge** ("Look at these two scratch cards. One's a winner…"). **None** open with "Why does…?"
2. **"You see,"** is his signature connector: 15 of 20 use it, several twice (once for setup, once
   before the mechanism). It sounds like a friend leaning in.
3. **The audience's doubt, spoken by a character.** 12 of 20 have a mocking or skeptical line
   spoken by a character: "Sir, that's impossible." / "Did your frogs get hungry again?" / "Pal, that horse
   lost a race to the milk cart, and it was *pulling* the milk cart." This voices the viewer's own
   objection, makes it funny, and sets up the reversal.
4. **Underdog → vindication.** The doubted person turns out right in about 10 of 20. "The whole
   court burst out laughing" → "the palace was a pile of rubble." "Nobody's laughing anymore."
5. **Very specific numbers as hooks and texture.** 82 holes, every 39 feet, one pint into a gallon,
   exactly 15 minutes, 47 scratches → 48, 1 inch to the left, 3:00 a.m., 300 lb, 10 ft past where he
   gave up. A precise number makes a strange act sound deliberate.
6. **A comic simile or exaggeration about every 10 s.** "like a human umbrella," "crushed like a
   soda can," "snap like a twig," "lit up like a giant lantern," "jockey is waving to his mom,"
   "lose a race to the saloon against a guy carrying two beers." These keep it light while the
   tension builds.
7. **The everyday-analogy bridge** for the science: soup ripples (oil on waves), catching a falling
   phone (reaction vs decision), a car on a hairpin at 5 vs 60 mph (loop shape). The viewer already
   has the experience; he just points at it.
8. **Time-stamped escalation.** "Next morning," "That night it pours," "Weeks later," "Then one
   night," "By the end of the month." Each marker is a new scene, so it implies a new shot.
9. **Archetypes, not names.** Mostly "he," "his buddy," "the rich guy," "the expert," "the quiet
   guy," "his rival," "the sheriff." Names appear only 3 times (Gary, Jerry, Slow Eddie). An
   archetype explains itself with no setup time.
10. **Second-person pulls.** "Imagine you're standing in the street at noon…," "Imagine your boss
    walks you past your desk…," "your hand." Used when the mechanism is about *your* body or brain.
11. **Ironic twist ending.** The schemer is caught by his own method (the dashcam, the undercover
    clerk). The sold thing contains the buyer (the knight robs the forgiveness seller). The doubter's
    "perfect" version fails at the worst moment (the railroad, the roller coaster).
12. **Hard stop.** Three transcripts end **mid-word** ("…how the dashcam was—," "…does the exact—,"
    "…onto the pirates'—"). None ask for a follow or say goodbye. My guess is the abrupt end is meant
    to make the video loop into its opening. I need the actual video to confirm that.

## 4. What wins on this channel (views vs. format)

| Tier | Videos | Common thread |
|---|---|---|
| **13–35M** | Ancient Jar 35M, Perfect Railroads 30M, Japan Job 18.6M, Storm Oil 16.8M, Grave Bell 13.7M, Roller Coaster 12.1M | **Real history/physics mechanism** + a mocked inventor or underdog + a big visual disaster at the end (palace rubble, rail kicking sideways, giant wave) |
| **8–11M** | Pink Cell, Burglar, Cannonballs, Cashiers, Second Draw, Cart, Crash | Real mechanism or real scheme, but a smaller final image or a list format |
| **< 7M** | Tickets, Mine Salting, Mirror, Dashcam, Button 3.8M, Knight 1.7M, Memory 0.57M | Pure fiction (Button, Memory), too long (Memory 78 s), too short (Knight 41 s), or a con-artist story with no physics |

Takeaways:
- **A real mechanism beats pure fiction** by about 5–10×. The two fully invented stories are at the
  bottom.
- **~60–65 s is the sweet spot.** The 41 s and 78 s videos are the two weakest.
- **The top two never name the science.** "Railroads" never says "thermal expansion," and "Jar"
  never says "seismograph." The picture shows it and the viewer feels clever.

## 5. How this compares to our CLAUDE.md formula

| Our rule | His practice | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Hook **question** in 2 s ("Why does every rich person…?") | **Statement of an absurd act** in under 3 s | Switch. A question invites a guess; a strange act demands an explanation. |
| "It's not X. It's Y. Here's how it works." early | Withholds the "how" until about 65% | Switch. Our reframe gives away the answer at second 4. |
| Named character (Victor) | Mostly faceless archetypes | Either works; archetypes save words. |
| Numbered steps + step tracker | Time markers ("next morning…") | Keep steps for multi-part money mechanics, but tell them as scenes, not a list. |
| "The catch / one risk" | The doubter + false "you were wrong" beat | Keep the honesty beat, but give it to a skeptic character. |
| Receipt / math card | Mechanism told through an everyday analogy, then one payoff image | Keep a receipt only when the numbers *are* the payoff. |
| Relatable contrast (teacher, nurse) | Second-person "Imagine you…" | Similar; his version is more immersive. |
| Punchline + "Follow for more ___" | Ironic payoff line, **hard stop**, no CTA | Test dropping the CTA. |
| 150–175 words | ~229 words at ~220 wpm | Raise to 200–230 words and speed the voice up. |
| Every sentence visualizable | Same, and each sentence is a **new physical action** | Same rule, enforced harder: an action verb in every sentence. |

## 6. Demo: Buy-Borrow-Die in his structure (script draft, no audio made)

> **He borrowed ten million dollars and never paid a cent of tax on it.** You see, Victor owns
> a hundred million in stock he bought for one million. Selling would trigger a tax bill of about
> twenty-three million, so he never sells a single share. Instead he walks into a bank and asks
> for a loan, using the stock as collateral. The banker frowns. "You're a hundred times richer than
> me and you want *my* money?" But loans aren't income, so there's no tax on them. And while he
> spends, the stock keeps growing faster than the interest piles up. Every few years he borrows
> again. His accountant begs him to sell, just once. He won't. Then Victor dies. His heirs inherit
> the shares, and the IRS resets their value to the price on the day he died. Thirty years of
> gains: never taxed. They sell just enough to pay back the bank, and keep the rest. You see, the
> tax isn't skipped. It's just waited out until it disappears.

*(About 170 words. It would be stretched to about 210 with more scene beats. The numbers and tax
claims must be checked against `videos/buy-borrow-die/sources.md` before any audio is made. "Never
paid tax" is simplified: estate tax can still apply. That needs a qualifying line or a source.)*

## 7. Visuals: what the frames show (15 stills from the top 5)

**Art style: 2D cartoon characters placed in fully 3D, lit worlds.**
- **Characters** are flat, hand-drawn figures: big round white heads, dot or ring eyes, thick black
  outlines, stick limbs, and a single soft gradient on bodies and clothes. All personality comes from
  the **face**: crying-laughing emperor, smug bowler-hat boss, sweating neighbor. Costume tells you
  the role at once (helmet = guard, top hat = rich, chef hat = cook, conductor cap).
- **Worlds** are real 3D scenes with stylized, low-poly materials and real lighting: a castle wall
  with a moonlit sea, a canyon with layered orange rock, a glass-roofed train station, an open ocean,
  an office, a small-town garden. A few **hero objects** are rendered richly in 3D: the bronze dragon
  jar has metallic gold PBR material, sculpted scales and a sun flare behind it.

**Lighting and color**
- **Every scene has a color temperature that matches its mood:** moonlit blue night (castle),
  golden-hour orange and pink (canyon, graveyard), deep navy storm (sea), cool white daylight (office)
  turning to blue night with a warm lamp cone at the low point of the story.
- **Warm against cool in nearly every frame:** orange torches against a blue night; an orange canyon
  against teal and blue clothes; a warm sunset behind the gold jar against green fields; a warm porch
  lantern against a blue night.
- **Dark scenes are never crushed to black.** Night is mid-blue, so shapes stay readable on a phone.
  This is the opposite of our gold-noir black void.
- **Light effects are saved for the big moments:** volumetric light shafts and a rim-lit rogue wave
  at the storm-oil climax, sun bloom behind the dragon jar. Ordinary story frames are evenly lit.
- **Saturation is high everywhere.** Primary reds, yellows and blues, like a kids' animated series.
- **Shadows are soft and low-contrast.** Contrast comes from color and value, not deep shadows.

**Composition and camera (from the stills)**
- **Varied angles even in 3 frames:** a symmetrical low angle on the throne, first-person POV with
  the viewer's own hand and phone in frame (Japan), a viewer's-eye view looking up the gravel at the
  rail (Railroads), a tilted wide-angle shot of the conductor, a deep wide shot with a crowd reaching
  into the distance (Grave Bell), a macro close-up of the jar.
- **Foreground framing creates depth:** rocks and gravel, the rail, a dug grave, a porch post in front
  of the subject.
- **Characters sit roughly in the middle third; the top 15% is sky or ceiling** (it gets covered by
  app UI anyway).

**Information lives inside the world, not on cards.** No overlay cards, counters or trackers in any
frame. Facts are props: a station banner "FIRST LINE IN GETS IT ALL!", a wall calendar "APR MON 7",
a phone showing "Inbox (0) — No new mail", a "JULY" calendar on a toolbox, and the **rail glowing
yellow to pink to show heat** (thermal expansion explained with color, never named).

**Captions:** one word at a time (sometimes two: "a cannonball," "six and"), white heavy sans-serif
with a **thick black stroke and drop shadow**, centered at about **65% of the screen height** (about
y 1250 of 1920). That's the same slot and the same one-word style as our engine. The difference is
the heavy outline, which keeps it readable over any background, white figures included.

### What this means for our engine
| Theirs | Ours today | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Bright, saturated, mood-colored 3D worlds | Black void, gold wireframes | Add lit, colored environment looks (the planned parchment/blueprint looks are a start; we need daylight, sunset and night presets) |
| Expressive cartoon characters | Faceless white blobs | Characters need faces and costumes. Three.js can render flat-shaded figures with outlines and swappable face textures |
| Diegetic text (banners, calendars, phone screens) | Floating DOM cards | Add "prop text" builders: sign, banner, calendar, phone, newspaper, receipt *in* the scene |
| Warm/cool lighting rigs, light shafts at the climax | One ambient light + bloom on everything | Per-shot lighting presets; save volumetric and bloom for the payoff |
| Outlined captions | Plain captions (the white-on-white problem) | Thick black stroke + shadow on captions — quick fix |

Our asset hosts (kenney.nl, quaternius.com, poly.pizza) already have low-poly environment kits in
this style, so the worlds are within reach. Characters are the hard part. And a caution: learn the
principles, but don't copy his character design. Our figures should be recognizably ours.

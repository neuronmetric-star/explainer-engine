- **Caveat:** the four newest videos (8–15 Nov) are under a week old and are still gaining views, so their low ratios are not yet meaningful. Subscriber and view history over time was unavailable.

**Topic and packaging [Data + Inference]**
- Subjects are small, physical, everyday-or-historical mechanisms with a hidden reason: rail gaps, storm oil, grave bells, pink cells, roller-coaster loop shapes, Japanese loopholes. Almost none needs prior knowledge.
- Titles are 4–8 words, always in Title Case, built on a concrete noun plus a mystery verb: “The Ancient Jar That *Warned* an Empire”, “Why Perfect Railroads *Don't Work*”, “This Shape *Saved* Roller Coasters”. There are few numbers and no clickbait caps.
- Video descriptions are just the title repeated. YouTube keywords are generic defaults. **[Inference]** Packaging depends entirely on the title and the opening line.

**Script and story [Data from transcripts]**
- **First line = a strange action, already in progress, by an unnamed “he”:** “He put a bronze ball in a dragon's mouth…”, “He saw gaps into a railroad every 39 feet”, “He poured cooking oil into the sea…”. Second-person variants: “Imagine your boss walks you past your desk…”, “Gambling is illegal in Japan. Yet…”.
- **The doubter arc:** an authority mocks the odd choice (“You're wrecking my railroad”, “you're making soup?”). The odd choice is then proven right in a dramatic moment. The mechanism is explained in 2–3 sentences right at the end.
- **The pivot phrase “You see,”** opens the explanation in 7 of 10 transcripts. This is a distinctive verbal tic of this creator; **do not copy it.**
- **The ending is the reveal itself.** There's no call to action or moral, just one image: “on paper, nobody gambled”, “you wake up holding the idea instead of the balls”.
- **Density [Inference, approximate word counts]:** about 170–200 words per minute, with very short clauses and present tense. Near-zero numbers or jargon; the science term is often never named (railroads never says “thermal expansion”).
- **Hedging:** “Edison was rumored to…” shows some care. But dramatized history with invented dialogue (Jar, Railroads) is presented without a disclaimer.

**Editing and visuals [Inference — not observed directly]**
- Instagram hashtags (#2danimation) and the bio (“original micro-animations”) indicate original **2D animation**. YouTube's “Made with AI” flag is **false**, and the transcripts show music cues. Style, frame rate and caption design could not be checked.
- Scenes implied by the scripts change on nearly every clause (≈ every 2–3 s), with recurring stock figures: a boss or captain, a crowd that laughs, a lone hero.
- **[Data]** Auto-dubbed into 7 languages, which may help global reach.

**Five recurring creative choices**
1. A cold open on an absurd action, with no setup.
2. A skeptic or crowd who mocks the hero.
3. Delayed reveal: the mechanism is explained only in the final 10–15 s.
4. A one-line image ending with no call to action.
5. A short, plain-language “why/how” title with an object as the subject.

**Three patterns linked to stronger sampled videos [correlation only]**
1. A **visible vindication moment** (palace collapses, rail buckles, wave hits pirates): present in 4 of the top 4.
2. **Real-world loophole or system** framed in 2nd person (Japan job, Japan gambling): both above 1.6×. Japan job drew 11K comments, plausibly because it is debatable.
3. **Length 58–67 s** with a single mechanism. The 31–35 s question-led videos are the lowest in the sample.

**Two less effective or inconsistent things**
1. **Question hooks with no character** (“How do painkillers know where the pain is?”, slot machines) landed at 0.01–0.03×.
2. **Pure fiction or world-building** (Memory, 78 s) and list-style escalation (Tree) underperform. The format seems to depend on a real hidden mechanism.

**Video formula:** *Odd action (0–3 s) → stakes and a skeptic (3–20 s) → doubled-down persistence and an escalating threat (20–45 s) → visible payoff + 2–3 sentence mechanism (45–60 s) → one-line image ending.*

**Best three examples:** [Ancient Jar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VS1Z_Q-Klt8) · [Railroads](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xS87iActkU) · [Storm Oil](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mz72MuPgnGY)

**Common ground [Inference]:**
- Both turn explanation into **story with a protagonist**.
- Both front-load the most extreme moment.
- Both use plain words over jargon.
- Both rely on drawn animation instead of stock footage.
- Both auto-dub for global reach.

**What's distinct:**
- Rick sells *curiosity about a mechanism*. Crayon sells *proximity to famous wealth and power*.
- Rick withholds the explanation. Crayon summarizes the whole arc up front, then promises the “how”.

**What could combine:**
- Crayon's **numbers-and-stakes** specificity inside Rick's **60-second mini-plot with a payoff reveal**.
- Crayon's *receipt-style proof* and Rick's *visible vindication* both fit this repo's step tracker and receipt systems.

**What depends on their identity or resources:**
- Rick's character-voice performance and an animation pipeline that can produce a short every 1–2 days.
- Crayon's verified channel, 314K subscribers, budget for long-form scripts, and an audience that already searches for famous names.
- A new channel has none of these.

## 5. Original channel playbook

### Positioning statement
**Gold-standard explainers about the hidden machinery of money and history: one surprising object, one real mechanism, one minute.** Each video starts at the strangest moment of a true story, follows a faceless character through 2–3 visual steps, and ends on proof: a receipt, a number, or a before/after that makes the viewer see an everyday thing differently. The look is our own: glowing gold wireframes on black for money, parchment for history, blueprint for science. Every claim is sourced, so viewers can trust the reveal.

### 30–60 s structure

| Time | Beat | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 s | **Hook:** a strange true fact stated as a scene, with a striking image already moving | Open a curiosity gap |
| 2–6 s | **Reframe:** what the viewer assumes vs. what's really going on | Promise a payoff |
| 6–12 s | **Character + stakes:** a faceless, named stand-in enters the situation | Give someone to follow |
| 12–40 s | **2–3 numbered steps,** one sentence of setup + one of payoff each; a new visual every 2–3 s | Teach the mechanism by showing it |
| 40–48 s | **The catch / twist:** an honest limit, a cost, or a surprising consequence | Build trust and add tension |
| 48–56 s | **Proof:** a real number or receipt on screen | Make it checkable |
| 56–60 s | **Punchline** that reframes something the viewer owns/uses + short CTA | Reward and loop |

### Hook-writing checklist
- [ ] Is it **true and sourced**?
- [ ] Is there a **concrete noun** in the first 5 words (coin, stone, ship, pill)?
- [ ] Does it contain a **contradiction** or a missing reason?
- [ ] Can it be **shown** in frame 1?
- [ ] Is it under **14 words**?
- [ ] Does it avoid “In today's video”, “Did you know”, and the borrowed tics above?

**10 original hook patterns**
1. **The object in your pocket:** “Look at the edge of a quarter. Those lines are a crime-prevention device.”
2. **Price of the impossible:** “This rock was worth a house, and nobody ever moved it.”
3. **The rule that backfired:** “A law meant to save money ended up doubling the bill.”
4. **The invisible fee:** “Every time you tap your card, three companies get paid.”
5. **Wrong job title:** “The most important person in a 1600s bank was a goldsmith.”
6. **Smallest cause, biggest effect:** “One missing digit can stop a payment.”
7. **Time-travel price tag:** “In 1700, this spoon cost a month's wages.”
8. **Two-word contradiction:** “Profitable garbage. Rome taxed it.”
9. **The thing still guarding you:** “Your coins are still defending against a thief from 1690.”
10. **The number that's lying:** “That 0% loan has a price. It's just written somewhere else.”

### Visual and editing checklist
- [ ] Frame 1 is the most striking image and is already moving (no fade-in).
- [ ] Something changes every 2–3 s: a camera move, card pop, counter, or object morph.
- [ ] One idea per shot, with each set at its own location (x = 0, 20, 40…).
- [ ] At most 2 cards plus the step tracker. Nothing in the caption zone (y 1250–1450), the top 140 px or the bottom 380 px.
- [ ] Fixed color meaning: gold = wealth/key term, red = loss/danger, white = facts, green = cash, blue = ordinary person.
- [ ] Big numbers use `cls:'big'`. Every number on screen matches `sources.md`.
- [ ] Sound: one low music bed, a soft “tick” on step changes, and one impact sound on the reveal only.
- [ ] End on the receipt/summary, then the FOLLOW button.
- [ ] Check the preview frame before the full render.

### Reusable script template
```
# HOOK (0–2s): [concrete object] + [contradiction]
[#hook] <one sentence, ≤14 words>
# REFRAME (2–6s)
[#reframe] It's not <assumption>. It's <real mechanism, in plain words>.
# CHARACTER (6–12s)
[#char] Meet <name>. <one-line situation with stakes>.
# STEPS (12–40s)
[#s1] Step one: <setup>. <payoff>.
[#s2] Step two: <setup>. <payoff>.
[#s3] Step three: <setup>. <payoff>.
# CATCH (40–48s)
[#catch] There's one catch: <honest limit or twist>.
# PROOF (48–56s)
[#proof] <real number + source-able fact>.
# PUNCHLINE + CTA (56–60s)
[#punch] <one line that reframes something the viewer owns>.
[#cta] Follow for more <series name>.
```

### Five original concepts

**1. The Coin at the Bottom of the Sea**
- **Premise:** On Yap, a giant stone “coin” that sank in a storm was still treated as wealth, because ownership lived in people's shared memory.
- **Hook:** “The richest family on this island kept its money at the bottom of the ocean.”
- **Learn:** money is a shared record, not a physical object.
- **Beats:** stone disc → quarried ~400 km away → stone sinks → still counted → ownership by agreement → Friedman's gold-vault parallel → your bank balance.
- **Visuals:** gold wireframe stone wheel, ocean floor, a ledger of names glowing above it.
- **Why it may hold:** a paradox in the first line, and the reveal maps to the viewer's own bank app.

**2. Why Your Coins Have Ridges**
- **Premise:** The lines on a quarter's edge began as a defense against people shaving silver off coins.
- **Hook:** “Run your thumb along a quarter. Those ridges were built to stop thieves.”
- **Learn:** clipping, the 1696 recoinage, Newton at the Mint, why pennies are smooth.
- **Beats:** smooth silver coin → clipper shaves → silver lump → treason → milled edges → U.S. silver until 1965 → ridges today.
- **Visuals:** macro coin edge, slivers piling into a gold lump, a red “TREASON” stamp.
- **Why it may hold:** viewers can check it with an object in their pocket, plus a celebrity cameo (Newton).

**3. The Last Digit Is a Snitch**
- **Premise:** The final digit of a card number is a check digit (Luhn, patented 1960) that catches most typos before a payment goes through.
- **Hook:** “The last number on your card isn't random. It's checking the others.”
- **Learn:** check digits, single-digit error detection.
- **Beats:** cashier mistypes → declined instantly → doubling rule shown on screen → sum ends in 0 → limits (not security; can't catch every swap).
- **Visuals:** blueprint-style digits sliding, doubling, summing to a green check.
- **Why it may hold:** viewers can try the math on their own card number (advise them to use a sample one).

**4. The Coffee Shop That Insures the World**
- **Premise:** Lloyd's of London grew out of Edward Lloyd's coffee house, where merchants traded shipping news and wrote insurance.
- **Hook:** “Some of the world's biggest insurance deals trace back to a coffee shop.”
- **Learn:** risk pooling, underwriting.
- **Beats:** 1688 notice → captains and merchants trade news → one ship's risk split among many backers → 1774 move to the Royal Exchange.
- **Visuals:** parchment diorama, ship miniature, names stacking under a risk slip.
- **Why it may hold:** familiar object (coffee) + huge outcome.

**5. The Emperor Who Taxed Pee**
- **Premise:** Vespasian taxed urine collected from public urinals, which launderers bought to clean wool, and defended it with “money doesn't smell”.
- **Hook:** “Ancient Rome made money from urine, and the emperor sniffed the coins to prove it.” *(Suetonius: he held a coin to his son's nose; adjust the wording to match.)*
- **Learn:** taxing by-products, how a tax base works.
- **Beats:** public urinals → fullers buy urine → tax on the buyers → Titus objects → coin test → the phrase survives today.
- **Visuals:** parchment Rome, a gold coin under Titus's nose, a laundry vat.
- **Why it may hold:** gross-out curiosity, and it's real.

**Fact-check notes**
- **“Anti-theft device from the 1600s”:** milled edges existed on some coins before 1696. Newton's Mint standardised them during the Great Recoinage, which is why the script says England struck new ridged coins in 1696 rather than “Newton invented ridges”. ⚠
- **Pennies and nickels:** the script says they were “cheap metal” rather than “never had silver”, because wartime 1942–45 nickels contained 35% silver (verify). ⚠
- **Clipping as treason with the death penalty** is supported by several sources (e.g. the APS on Newton and William Chaloner). Exact statutes vary by era. ⚠
- **The U.S. Mint says dimes were reeded so people wouldn't file off silver.** The Mint's spec sheet lists 118 reeds on the dime and 119 on the quarter. Re-confirm on usmint.gov. ⚠
- **The Coinage Act of 1965** removed silver from dimes and quarters. ⚠ Primary source: Public Law 89-81.

# Sources: Why people want what others have

**Victor, the G-Wagon driver, the little-car driver, Doug, the man in the wheelchair, the phone wallpaper and the
red light are fictional** (Brian's story). The psychology is real.

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| "Your brain has no ruler for enough. It just compares you to what's next to you": social comparison theory. Without an objective standard, people judge their own abilities and standing by comparing themselves with others, usually similar others | Festinger, L. (1954), "A Theory of Social Comparison Processes," *Human Relations* 7(2): 117–140. PDF: https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/%7Eschaller/528Readings/Festinger1954.pdf · overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_comparison_theory |
| Same idea with money: people feel worse when the people *around them* earn more, even when their own income stays the same | Luttmer, E. F. P. (2005), "Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 120(3): 963–1002. NBER version: https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667 ("controlling for an individual's own income, higher earnings of neighbors are associated with lower levels of self-reported happiness") |
| Background, not in the narration (optional swap, see below): in a Harvard School of Public Health survey of 257 students and staff, about half (48% for income) preferred earning $50,000 while others earn $25,000 over $100,000 while others earn $200,000, with prices the same | Solnick, S. J. & Hemenway, D. (1998), "Is more always better?: A survey on positional concerns," *J. of Economic Behavior & Organization* 37(3): 373–383: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeborg/v37y1998i3p373-383.html · Harvard Magazine write-up (48% figure; seen via search, page didn't load from here): https://harvardmagazine.com/sites/default/files/html/1996/mj96/right.up.html · Laurie Santos (Yale) on the same question: https://www.drlauriesantos.com/newsletter/do-you-want-to-be-rich-or-just-richer-than-everyone-you-know |
| "Once you get it, it turns normal… the hedonic treadmill: you run and run, and go nowhere": after good or bad events, happiness tends to drift back toward where it was. The treadmill metaphor is Brickman & Campbell's (1971) | Psychology Today, "Hedonic Treadmill": https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/basics/hedonic-treadmill · Wikipedia, "Hedonic treadmill" (incl. Diener, Lucas & Scollon 2006: the return is not always complete): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonic_treadmill |
| Supporting study: major lottery winners were not significantly happier than ordinary people, and enjoyed everyday pleasures less | Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978), "Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?" *JPSP* 36(8): 917–927: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/690806/ |
| "a Lamborghini": an Italian sports-car maker (Sant'Agata Bolognese) | https://www.lamborghini.com/en-en/company |
| "a G-Wagon, a giant Mercedes": nickname for the boxy Mercedes-Benz G-Class SUV | https://www.mbusa.com/en/vehicles/class/g-class/suv |

**Care note:** the man in the wheelchair is shown with respect: a calm, ordinary guy watching, not a punchline.
The narration doesn't claim people who use wheelchairs are unhappy. (Brickman 1978 found the accident group less happy
than the controls but far from miserable, and they tended to idealize their past, which fits "I wish I could walk again.")

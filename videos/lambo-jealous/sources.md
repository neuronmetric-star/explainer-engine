# Sources: Why the heck is a guy in a Lamborghini jealous?

**Fiction:** Victor, the G-Wagon driver, the regular-car driver, Doug and the man in the wheelchair are invented
characters, and the red-light scene is a made-up story (based on Brian's script). The psychology and the prices are real.

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| "His Lamborghini, worth seven regular cars": the 2026 Lamborghini Temerario (the entry Lamborghini) starts around $360,000–$387,000 in the US (Motor1: $361,616 incl. destination; KBB: $386,649). The average new car sold for a record $51,109 (Kelley Blue Book, reported Nov 10, 2026). $361,616 ÷ $51,109 ≈ 7.1 (**our calculation**) | Motor1, "How Much Does a Lamborghini Actually Cost?": https://www.motor1.com/features/344294/how-much-is-a-lamborghini/ · KBB Temerario: https://www.kbb.com/lamborghini/temerario/ · NPR/WAMC, "The average cost for a new vehicle in the U.S. hits a record high over $51,000": https://www.wamc.org/2026-11-10/the-average-cost-for-a-new-vehicle-in-the-u-s-hits-a-record-high-over-51-000 |
| "a G-Wagon, a Mercedes shaped like a shoebox": the Mercedes-Benz G-Class (nicknamed G-Wagon) is a boxy luxury SUV; the 2026 G 550 starts at $153,900 (≈ 3 regular cars; not said aloud) | Cars.com, 2026 G-Class trims: https://www.cars.com/research/mercedes_benz-g_class-2026/trims · KBB G-Class 2026: https://www.kbb.com/mercedes-benz/g-class/2026/ |
| (picture only) a helicopter costs more than the Lamborghini: Robinson R44, one of the most common small helicopters, lists at $520,000–$646,000 (Jan 1, 2026 price list, via dealer listings) | Controller.com listing, 2026 Robinson R44 Raven II: https://www.controller.com/listing/for-sale/256351419/2026-robinson-r44-raven-ii-piston-helicopters |
| "Your brain doesn't measure what you have. It measures whoever's one step above you": social comparison theory (Leon Festinger, 1954): people judge themselves by comparing with others, with a drive to compare *upward* on abilities; later research on "upward comparison" links it to envy and dissatisfaction | Festinger (1954), "A Theory of Social Comparison Processes", summarized: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_comparison_theory · Psychology Today, Social Comparison Theory: https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/social-comparison-theory · EBSCO Research Starters: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/psychology/social-comparison-theory |
| "And whatever you get, you get used to": hedonic adaptation / the "hedonic treadmill" (Brickman & Campbell, 1971). Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978): 22 big lottery winners were **not significantly happier** than 22 non-winners (4.00 vs 3.82 on a 0–5 scale) and took **less** pleasure in everyday things | PubMed, "Lottery winners and accident victims: is happiness relative?" (J Pers Soc Psychol, 1978): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/690806/ · Wikipedia, Hedonic treadmill: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonic_treadmill · Diener, Lucas & Scollon (2006), *Beyond the Hedonic Treadmill* (adaptation is real but not total): https://www.wisebrain.org/papers/BeyondHedonicTreadmill.pdf |
| (context, not said) about 5.5 million Americans use a wheelchair or mobility scooter (US Census Bureau figure, as reported) | HealthDay: https://www.healthday.com/health-news/disabilities/fear-of-falls-injuries-can-plague-motorized-wheelchair-users · Harvard Health: https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/millions-rely-on-wheelchairs-for-mobility-but-repair-delays-are-hurting-users-202207212785 |
| (backs the closing question) "What's one thing you have today that you used to wish for?": counting blessings. Emmons & McCullough (2003): people randomly assigned to write down things they were grateful for each week showed higher well-being than people listing hassles | PubMed, "Counting blessings versus burdens": https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12585811/ · Full paper (Greater Good, UC Berkeley): https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/pdfs/GratitudePDFs/6Emmons-BlessingsBurdens.pdf |

**Caveats:** Lamborghini US pricing varies by source ($290k–$387k); "seven regular cars" uses the Motor1 figure and is
"about". The lottery study is small (22 winners) and the "treadmill" idea has since been softened: people adapt a lot,
not always completely. The script says "you get used to" (true on average), not "money can't buy happiness".

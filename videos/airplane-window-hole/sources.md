# Sources: airplane-window-hole ("Why the heck do airplane windows have a tiny hole?")

Researched Nov 23 2026 (web search). Search phrase: "why do airplane windows have a hole".

## Fiction / reconstruction (labeled)
- Doug building the window, Victor the inspector, Gary in the window seat: **invented characters**.
- The story order (one pane → backup pane → sealed airtight → fog/frost → drill the hole) is a **teaching reconstruction**,
  not how the window was really designed. The mechanism it reveals is real (below).
- Simplified on purpose: real cabin windows have **three** panes; the thin inner "scratch pane" passengers touch is left out
  (side trivia). The script's "backup pane" is the real **middle pane**, the one with the hole.

## Facts in the script
| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Cabin windows are clear stretched acrylic (plastic), not glass; three panes: outer, middle (with the hole), inner scratch pane | The Points Guy, "What are airplane windows made of": https://thepointsguy.com/airline/what-are-airplane-windows-made-of · AirHelp: https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/blog/airplane-window-hole/ |
| The hole ("breather"/"bleed" hole) is in the middle pane | Afar: https://www.afar.com/magazine/why-airplane-windows-have-tiny-holes · Time: https://time.com/3903109/airplane-window-holes |
| With the hole, the outer pane takes the cabin pressure; the middle pane is the fail-safe | Fortune: https://fortune.com/2015/06/01/airplane-window-holes/ · Global Air: https://www.globalair.com/articles/why-do-airplane-windows-have-tiny-holes?id=11063 |
| The hole puts cabin air on both sides of the middle pane (equalizes the gap with the cabin) | Time (above) · The Flying Engineer: https://theflyingengineer.com/airplane-window-hole/ |
| If the outer pane fails, the hole is too small for cabin air to escape quickly, so the middle pane takes the load (while pilots descend) | Migflug, "The Hole in Your Aircraft Window": https://migflug.com/afterburner/?p=13575130 · Fortune (above) |
| The hole lets moisture out of the gap, so the window doesn't fog; a sealed damp gap meeting a ~−55 °C pane would condense and freeze | Fox News: https://www.foxnews.com/travel/why-do-airplane-windows-have-tiny-holes · Migflug (above) · Time: https://time.com/4725944/tiny-hole-airplane-windows-travel |
| Airliners cruise around 35,000 ft (≈ 6.6 miles, "seven miles up") | Military & Aerospace Electronics, "Why commercial aircraft cruise around 35,000 feet": https://www.militaryaerospace.com/commercial-aerospace/article/55399427/why-commercial-aircraft-cruise-around-35000-feet |
| Air up there is too thin to breathe, so the cabin is pressurized (pumped full of air) | Wikipedia, "Cabin pressurization": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization · SkyBrary, "Cabin Altitude": https://skybrary.aero/articles/cabin-altitude |
| Inside pushes out about 8 psi more than outside at cruise ("a gallon of milk on every square inch") | Wikipedia (above; typical max differential ≈ 8 psi) · SkyBrary (above) · a US gallon of whole milk ≈ 8.6 lb: https://www.inchcalculator.com/milk-weight-calculator/ |
| Outside air at cruise ≈ −55 °C (−67 °F), "colder than a freezer" (home freezer ≈ −18 °C / 0 °F) | National Academies, "Health and Safety Impacts of Aircraft Cabin Temperatures": https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/29472/chapter/4 · AirHelp, "How high do planes fly": https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/blog/how-high-do-planes-fly/ |

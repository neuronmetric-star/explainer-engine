## Narration
Why do YOUR ears pop on a plane? They're not broken. There's a tiny door inside your head, and it's usually shut.

Behind your eardrum is a pocket of air. A skinny tube joins it to your throat, and the door sits at the end.

See, Doug had a problem. His passengers grabbed their ears on every flight. Even Gary.

Here's the thing about air: it has weight. The higher you go, the less air is stacked on top of you, so it pushes less. Carry a chip bag up a mountain and it puffs up, because the air outside pushes less. A climbing plane does the same: the air in the cabin thins out, so it pushes less too.

So the air behind your eardrum pushes it out. Then the door opens, a little air slips out, and... pop.

Coming down is worse.

Now the cabin air pushes more, squeezing your eardrum in. And the door often won't open fast enough. Ouch.

Doug's fix: swallow. Each swallow tugs the door open. Perfect! Except... Gary's asleep, and sleepers don't swallow. So Doug hands out gum. Chewing keeps you swallowing.

But why isn't it worse way up high? Doug pumps the cabin full of air, so it feels like a mountain town, not the top of the sky. That keeps the change small.

So that pop? It's the small version of a much bigger squeeze. Now Doug's passengers swallow.

Next flight, swallow on the way down, and feel the door open.

Send this to the friend who grabs their ears.

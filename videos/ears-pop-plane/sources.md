# Sources: ears-pop-plane ("Why do your ears pop on a plane?") · Plane Heck episode 3

Researched Nov 25 2026 (web search). Search phrase: "why do your ears pop on a plane".

## Fiction / reconstruction (labeled)
- Doug (airplane builder), Gary (passenger, asleep, with a cold) and every scene with them: **invented characters**; the story is a teaching device.
- The chip bag on a mountain and the drum skin are **teaching analogies**, not sourced claims.
- "A mountain town" = the cabin's air feels like about 8,000 ft up (the on-screen number is a number, not a place we name).

## Facts in the script
| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Behind the eardrum is a small air-filled middle ear; the Eustachian tube links it to the back of the nose and is normally closed; swallowing/yawning opens it, and the pop is the tube opening | Time: https://time.com/4901296/why-do-your-ears-pop-on-airplanes/ · Popular Science: https://www.popsci.com/science/why-do-ears-pop-on-plane/ · Merck Manuals: https://www.merckmanuals.com/home/multimedia/video/v26621636 |
| Climb: cabin pressure drops, middle-ear pressure is relatively higher, the eardrum bulges outward | Merck Manuals (above) |
| Descent: cabin pressure rises, the tube often stays shut, middle-ear pressure is relatively low, the eardrum is pulled inward; trouble is most common on descent | Merck Manuals (above) · Mayo Clinic, causes: https://www.mayoclinic.org/health/airplane-ear/DS00472/DSECTION=causes · Brown University Health Services: https://healthservices.brown.edu/sites/default/files/handouts/airplane-travel-ears2022.pdf |
| Yawn and swallow during takeoff and landing; gum or candy helps you swallow; gently blow with the nose pinched and mouth closed (Valsalva) | Mayo Clinic, prevention: https://www.mayoclinic.org/health/airplane-ear/DS00472/DSECTION=prevention · Cleveland Clinic: https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-to-pop-your-ears/ |
| Sleeping through ascent/descent is a risk factor because you don't swallow or yawn | Mayo Clinic: https://mayoclinic.org/health/airplane-ear/DS00472 |
| Colds, sinus infections and allergies can inflame the tube and make popping harder | Time and Popular Science (above) |
| Cabin pressure is held at no more than 8,000 ft equivalent at the plane's top altitude (14 CFR 25.841) | Cornell LII: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/25.841 · Federal Register 2023: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-06-15/html/2023-12454.htm |
| Cabin altitude is kept above sea level to reduce stress on the fuselage (stress grows with the pressure difference inside vs outside) | Wikipedia, Cabin pressurization: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization · migflug: https://migflug.com/afterburner/why-airplane-cabins-are-pressurised-to-6000-feet-not-sea-level/ |

- The mountain counter on the chip-bag shot (1,000 → 8,000 FT) is **illustrative**: it ends at the same number as the cabin rule (8,000 ft) to tie the two ideas together; it is not a measured bag test.

## Careful wording
- The pop is **the tube opening**; we say "a little air slips through", never "the ear cracks".
- We say "swallowing helps", not "gum fixes it": it's the swallowing (Cleveland Clinic).
- "Far bigger squeeze without the pumped-up cabin" is a plain-language inference from the pressure numbers; no figure is stated aloud.
- No medical advice: we explain how it works. Pain that doesn't go away is a doctor question (one line in the caption, not the video).
- Newer jets (787, A350) hold the cabin at about 6,000 ft; the script says "about a mile and a half up" only as an on-screen label tied to the 8,000 ft rule, so we say "up to".
